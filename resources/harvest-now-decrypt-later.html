What Is Harvest Now, Decrypt Later?

Why the migration deadline isn't "when quantum computers arrive."

The idea in one sentence

An adversary can record RSA- or ECC-encrypted traffic and data today, store it, and decrypt it later, the moment a quantum computer capable of running Shor's algorithm at sufficient scale exists — even though that computer doesn't exist yet.

Why this changes the timeline

For a lot of security problems, "we'll deal with it when it becomes a real threat" is a reasonable stance. Harvest now, decrypt later breaks that logic, because the attack has two separate clocks:

  • Collection — can happen right now, against any RSA- or ECC-protected data an adversary can intercept or exfiltrate, with no quantum computer required.
  • Decryption — happens later, whenever a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists.

If your data needs to stay confidential for five, ten, or twenty years, the question isn't whether a quantum computer exists today. It's whether one will exist before your data's confidentiality requirement expires — and whether the migration finishes before the collection window closes.

Who this actually matters for

Not all data is equally exposed. The organizations with the most urgency are the ones protecting information with a long shelf life:

  • Financial account and transaction records, often retained for years by regulation
  • Health records, which frequently need to stay confidential for decades
  • Government and defense communications and long-lived state secrets
  • Legal and privileged material tied to matters that can span years
  • Any long-lived cryptographic material itself — root keys, signing keys, certificate authorities — where compromise doesn't just expose one record, it undermines everything that key ever protected

What "collected now" actually requires

Harvest now, decrypt later doesn't require a breach of your systems in the way ransomware does. Encrypted traffic that transits a network an adversary can observe — an ISP, an internet exchange, a compromised intermediate hop — is enough to collect. The encryption itself, RSA or ECC, is exactly what's supposed to make that observation useless. Under this threat model, it isn't, on a long enough timeline.

What this means for a migration plan

The practical takeaway isn't "panic." It's "sequence by data lifetime." Start by finding where RSA and ECC actually protect your longest-lived, most sensitive data — not just where they're easiest to find — because that's the risk with the most urgent clock.
